# The Ringstone of Heart-Wisdom in the Word of Shuʿayb

*Ibn ʿArabī, Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam, chapter 12 (Faṣṣ ḥikma qalbiyya fī kalima shuʿaybiyya)*

*Translated from the Arabic of ʿAfīfī's edition (1946), via the OpenITI digital text; a few evident misreadings in the digital text are corrected silently. Square brackets give Arabic terms at first occurrence, Qurʾān references, and a few words supplied for sense. "God" renders Allāh and "the Real" renders al-Ḥaqq. "Self" renders dhāt and "soul" renders nafs, except where nafs is a plain reflexive ("himself," "itself"). Pronouns for God are lowercase throughout: the Arabic does not distinguish them, and the chapter exploits the ambiguity.*

---

Know that the heart [qalb][^1] — I mean the heart of one who recognizes God [al-ʿārif bi-llāh][^2] — is of God's compassion [raḥma],[^3] and yet it is vaster than compassion. For it encompasses the Real [al-Ḥaqq] (majestic is his majesty), whereas his compassion does not encompass him.[^4] This is the language of the common folk, by way of allusion: the Real shows compassion; he is not shown it, so compassion has no rule over him.

As for the allusion in the language of the elect: God described himself [nafsahu] as having breath [nafas], and breath comes from relieving [tanfīs].[^5] The divine names are the very Named, and there is nothing but he. The names demand the very realities they bestow, and the realities the names demand are nothing other than the world. Divinity [ulūhiyya] demands a worshipper [maʾlūh], and lordship [rubūbiyya] demands a servant [marbūb];[^ML] they have no entity [ʿayn] except through the world, whether in existence or in supposition. Yet the Real, with respect to his self [dhāt],[^DH] is "independent [ghanī] of the worlds" [Q 3:97]. Lordship does not have this property. So the matter hung between what lordship demands and the independence from the world that the self merits — and yet lordship, in reality and as a qualification, is nothing other than this very self. When the matter was thus caught in conflict by virtue of the relations, there came in the reports what the Real described himself with: tender pity [shafaqa] for his servants. So the first relief he gave to lordship was through his breath, which is ascribed to al-Raḥmān [the All-Compassionate]:[^6] he brought into existence the world that lordship demands by its very reality, as do all the divine names.

From this angle it is established that "his compassion encompasses all things" [Q 7:156], and so it encompasses the Real; it is thus vaster than the heart, or equal to it in vastness. So much for that.

Next, know that the Real (exalted is he), as is established in the Ṣaḥīḥ, transmutes from form to form when he shows himself;[^7] and that when the heart encompasses the Real, it can encompass no created thing along with him — it is as though he fills it. The meaning of this is that when the heart looks at the Real as he shows himself to it, it cannot look at anything else along with him. The vastness of the heart of one who recognizes [the Real] is as Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī said: "Were the Throne and all it contains a hundred million times over set in one corner of the heart of one who recognizes, he would not feel it." And al-Junayd said in the same sense: "When the newly arrived [muḥdath] is joined to the eternal [qadīm], no trace of it remains." How then could a heart that encompasses the eternal sense the newly arrived as existing?

Since the Real's autophany [tajallī][^8] varies in forms, the heart necessarily widens and narrows according to the form in which the divine autophany occurs, for nothing of it is left over beyond the form in which the autophany occurs. The heart of one who recognizes, or of the perfect human being, is like the setting for the ringstone in a ring:[^9] nothing is left over; it takes the stone's measure and shape — round if the stone is round, or square, hexagonal, octagonal, or any other shape if the stone is square, hexagonal, octagonal, or whatever shape it may be. Its setting in the ring is exactly like it, and nothing else.

This is the reverse of what the Folk [the Sufis] point to when they say that the Real shows himself according to the servant's preparedness [istiʿdād]. That is not how it is. Rather, the servant appears to the Real according to the form in which the Real shows himself to him.

The precise statement of the question is this. God has two autophanies: an autophany of the unseen [ghayb] and an autophany of the witnessed [shahāda]. By the autophany of the unseen he gives the preparedness the heart will have. This is the autophany of the self [al-tajallī al-dhātī], whose reality is the unseen; it is what "he" refers to [huwiyya], which is his by right when he says of himself, "he."[^10] So "he" is his, always and forever. When the heart has obtained this preparedness, he shows himself to it with the witnessing autophany, in the witnessed world; the heart sees him, and takes on the form of what showed itself to it, as we have said. Thus he (exalted is he) gave it its preparedness, as in his words, "He gave each thing its creation" [Q 20:50]. Then he lifted the veil between himself and his servant, and the servant saw him in the form of his conception [muʿtaqad]: what he sees is his very belief [iʿtiqād].[^11]

Neither the heart nor the eye ever witnesses anything but the form of its conception of the Real. The Real who is in the conception is the one whose form the heart encompasses; he is the one who shows himself to it, and so it recognizes him. The eye sees nothing but the Real of belief.

The diversity of beliefs is no secret. Whoever binds him [qayyadahu] denies him in anything other than what he has bound him to, and acknowledges him in what he has bound him to, when he shows himself there. But whoever releases him from binding [aṭlaqahu ʿan al-taqyīd] does not deny him; he acknowledges him in every form into which he transmutes, and gives him, from his own soul, the measure of each form in which he shows himself to him — without end, for the forms of autophany have no limit at which they stop. So too, knowledge of God has no end in the one who recognizes, no point at which he might stop; rather, at every moment the one who recognizes seeks increase in knowledge of him: "My Lord, increase me in knowledge," "My Lord, increase me in knowledge," "My Lord, increase me in knowledge" [Q 20:114].[^12] So the matter is endless from both sides.

That is, if you speak of "Real" and "creation" [ḥaqq wa-khalq]. But if you consider his words, "I am his foot with which he walks, his hand with which he strikes, and his tongue with which he speaks"[^13] — and so on through the other faculties and their seat, the bodily members — you will make no division; you will say the matter is all Real, or all creation. It is creation in one relation and Real in another, and the entity is one. So the very form of what showed itself is the very form of the one who received that autophany: he is the one who shows himself and the one to whom he is shown. See, then, how wondrous God's affair is — as the one "he" refers to, and as related to the world through the realities of his most beautiful names.

> Who is there, and what is there?
> The entity of "there" — he is there.
>
> Whom he included in common, he singled out;
> whom he singled out, he included in common.
>
> There is no entity but the one entity —
> and the light of its eye is darkness.
>
> Whoever is heedless of this
> finds gloom [ghumma] within his soul;
>
> and none recognizes what we have said
> but a servant with concentrated intent [himma].[^14]

"In that there is a reminder for whoever has a heart" [Q 50:37] — because of its shifting [taqallub] through every kind of form and attribute. He did not say "for whoever has an intellect [ʿaql]," for intellect is a fetter:[^15] it confines the matter to a single description, while reality, in itself, refuses confinement. So it is not a reminder for those who have intellects. They are the people of beliefs, who declare one another unbelievers and curse one another, "and they have no helpers" [cf. Q 3:22, 30:29].

For the God of one conception has no authority over the God of another. The holder of a belief defends him — that is, defends what he believes about his God — and comes to his help; but the God in his belief does not come to his help, and that is why it has no effect on the belief of the one disputing with him. Likewise his opponent receives no help from the God in his own belief. So "they have no helpers": the Real has denied help to the Gods of beliefs, each conception taken singly and on its own. The one helped is the whole, and the helper is the whole.

So the Real, for the one who recognizes, is the one recognized [al-maʿrūf] and never denied [lā yunkar]. "The people of the recognized in this world are the people of the recognized in the next."[^16] That is why he said, "for whoever has a heart": such a one comes to know the Real's shifting in forms through being turned [taqlīb] through shapes himself. From his soul he recognized his soul. And his soul is not other than the one "he" refers to, the Real; nor is anything in the cosmos, of what is and what will be, other than the one "he" refers to — rather, it is that very one. So he is the one who recognizes, who knows, who acknowledges, in this form; and he is the one who neither recognizes nor knows, who denies, in that other form. This is the portion of one who recognizes the Real through autophany and witnessing in the very entity of union [ʿayn al-jamʿ]. This is what is meant by his words, "for whoever has a heart" — a heart that varies in its being turned.

As for the people of faith — those who follow on authority [muqallida], who follow the prophets and messengers in what they reported of the Real, not those who follow the people of speculative thought, and those who interpret away the transmitted reports by bending them to their rational proofs — these, who follow the messengers (God's blessings and peace be upon them), are the ones meant by his words, "or who gives ear" [Q 50:37], to what the divine reports brought on the tongues of the prophets (God's blessings and peace be upon them). "While being a witness [shahīd]" — that is, the one who gives ear is a witness — draws attention to the imaginal presence [ḥaḍrat al-khayāl][^17] and its use. This is what the Prophet (peace be upon him) said about excellence [iḥsān]: "that you worship God as if you see him."[^18] And God is in the prayer-direction [qibla] of the one who prays; that is why he is a witness. But whoever follows a person of speculative thought and binds himself to him is not one who "gives ear," for the one who gives ear must be a witness, for the reason we have given; and when he is not a witness in the way we have described, he is not the one meant by this verse. These are the ones of whom God said, "when those who were followed disown those who followed" [Q 2:166] — whereas the messengers do not disown those who followed them.

So realize [ḥaqqiq], my friend, what I have set out for you in this wisdom of the heart. As for its being assigned to Shuʿayb, that is because of the branching [tashaʿʿub] in it: its branches [shuʿab] cannot be numbered, since every belief is a branch. They are all branches — I mean the beliefs.[^19]

When the covering is lifted, things are unveiled for each person according to his conception; yet they may also be unveiled contrary to his conception as regards the ruling [ḥukm]. This is his saying, "there appeared to them from God what they had not reckoned with" [Q 39:47]. Most of this concerns rulings: like the Muʿtazilite, who believes of God that his threat of punishment will be carried out on the sinner who dies without repenting. If he dies and he is one to whom God shows compassion, for whom a prior providence has decreed that he will not be punished, he finds God "forgiving, compassionate" [ghafūr, raḥīm], and there appears to him from God what he had not reckoned with.

As for who "he" is: some servants are certain in their belief that God is such-and-such. When the covering is lifted, such a one sees the form of his conception, and it is real [ḥaqq], and he holds to it. Then the knot [ʿuqda] comes undone: belief falls away and becomes knowledge by direct witnessing.[^20] And once the sight has been sharpened, it does not return to dimness [cf. Q 50:22]. Then, because autophany differs across forms at the moment of vision — for it never repeats itself — something contrary to his conception may appear to some servants. So it holds true of him, as to who "he" is: "there appeared to them from God" — as to who he is — "what they had not reckoned with" regarding it before the covering was lifted.

We have described the form of ascent after death in divine recognitions in our *Book of Autophanies* [Kitāb al-Tajalliyāt], where we mention those of the Folk we met in unveiling, and what we taught them on this question that they did not already have.[^21] One of the most wondrous things is that a person is always ascending and does not sense it, because the veil is so subtle and fine and the forms resemble one another — as in his words, "and they will be given it in resemblance" [Q 2:25].[^22] Yet the one is not the very same as the other, for to one who recognizes, two things that resemble each other are, by that very fact, two different things.

The one of realization [ṣāḥib al-taḥqīq] sees multiplicity in the one, just as he knows that what the divine names denote — though their realities differ and multiply — is a single entity. This is an intelligible multiplicity within what is one in entity. So in autophany there is a witnessed multiplicity in a single entity — just as prime matter [hayūlā] enters the definition of every form, and, for all the multiplicity and difference of the forms, they go back in reality to a single substance [jawhar], which is their prime matter. Whoever recognizes his soul with this recognition has recognized his Lord,[^23] for he created him in his form[^24] — rather, he [the Lord] is the very one that person's "he" refers to, and his reality.

That is why none of the learned has hit upon the recognition of the soul and its reality except the divine ones among the messengers and the Sufis. As for the people of rational consideration and the masters of speculative thought, among the ancients and the theologians [mutakallimūn], in what they said about the soul and its essence [māhiyya] — not one of them hit upon its reality, nor will speculative consideration [naẓar fikrī] ever yield it. Whoever seeks knowledge of it by way of speculative thought has taken swelling for fat and blown on embers that were never lit.[^25] No wonder they are among "those whose effort goes astray in the life of this world while they reckon they are doing good work" [Q 18:104]. Whoever seeks the matter by any path but its own will not attain its realization.

How beautifully God spoke concerning the world and its being exchanged with every breath [anfās] in a new creation within a single entity! Concerning one group — no, most of the world — he said, "No, they are in confusion [labs] about a new creation" [Q 50:15]. They do not recognize the renewal of the matter with every breath.

The Ashʿarites, however, did hit upon it with respect to some existents — the accidents [aʿrāḍ]; and the Ḥisbāniyya [sceptics][^26] hit upon it with respect to the whole world. All the people of speculative thought charged them with ignorance. But both groups erred.

The Ḥisbāniyya erred in that, while holding that the whole world is exchanged, they did not hit upon the oneness of the entity of the substance that received this form — the substance that exists only through the form, just as the form is intelligible only through it. Had they affirmed that, they would have won the degree of realization in the matter.

As for the Ashʿarites, they did not know that the whole world is a collection of accidents, and so is exchanged at every moment, since an accident does not endure for two moments. This shows in their definitions of things. When they define a thing, their definition makes plain that it consists of accidents, and that the accidents mentioned in its definition are the very substance and its reality, self-subsistent — though each, insofar as it is an accident, does not subsist by itself. So from a collection of things that do not subsist by themselves there has come something that does: as with occupying space [taḥayyuz] in the definition of the self-subsistent substance — intrinsic [dhātī] to it — and its receiving accidents, which is likewise an intrinsic definition of it. There is no doubt that receiving is an accident, since it exists only in a receiver, because it does not subsist by itself; yet it is intrinsic to the substance. And occupying space is an accident that exists only in what occupies space, so it does not subsist by itself. Yet occupying space and receiving are nothing added to the very entity of the defined substance, for intrinsic definitions are the very thing defined, the thing itself.[^27] So what does not endure for two moments has come to endure for two moments and more, and what does not subsist by itself has come to subsist by itself — and they are not aware of the position they are in. These are the ones "in confusion about a new creation."

But the people of unveiling [ahl al-kashf] see that God shows himself in every breath and never repeats an autophany. And they see, by witnessing, that every autophany gives a new creation and takes away a creation. Its going away is the very passing-away [fanāʾ] at the autophany, and subsistence [baqāʾ] belongs to what the next autophany gives. So understand.

## Notes

[^1]: *Qalb*, "heart," comes from a root meaning "to turn, to turn over." The Arabic heart is by its very name the turning organ, and the chapter leans on this: the heart's *taqallub* (rendered "shifting") through forms mirrors the Real's shifting in forms, and God's *taqlīb* of the heart is rendered "being turned." Ordinary piety prays to God as "Turner of hearts" (*muqallib al-qulūb*) for a heart made firm; Ibn ʿArabī instead praises the heart's ceaseless shifting. An alchemical image fits: like quicksilver, which takes the exact shape of any vessel, the heart takes the shape of each autophany (see n. 9).

[^2]: *ʿĀrif*, with *maʿrifa* and the verb *ʿarafa*, is rendered throughout with the vocabulary of recognition, so that the chapter's logic of recognizing and denying (*ʿarafa / ankara*) stays audible in English. *ʿIlm* is rendered "knowledge."

[^3]: *Raḥma*, "compassion," shares its root with *raḥim*, "womb." In Ibn ʿArabī's usage it is above all the bestowal of existence: to be shown compassion is to be given being.

[^4]: The opening relies on a saying widely cited by Sufis (e.g. in al-Ghazālī's *Iḥyāʾ*), though absent from the canonical ḥadīth collections: "My earth and my heaven do not encompass me, but the heart of my believing servant encompasses me."

[^5]: A three-way pun. God describes his *nafs* as having *nafas* (breath), and *nafas* is connected with *tanfīs*, relieving a constriction, as a sigh relieves pent-up feeling. *Nafs* is "soul," and also the ordinary reflexive, "himself"; the translation gives the reflexive here and "soul" wherever the word stands as a noun. The divine names "demand" a world in which their effects can appear; the Breath relieves that pressure by breathing the world into existence. Later in the chapter *anfās*, "breaths," means "instants": creation is renewed "with every breath."

[^ML]: Literally "the god-ed" and "the lorded-over": *maʾlūh* and *marbūb* are passive forms built from *ilāh* (god) and *rabb* (lord), naming whatever a god is god to and a lord is lord of. The point is that these names are correlative: there is no lord without something lorded over, so lordship, unlike the self, needs a world. "Servant" here is not limited to human beings: "There is none in the heavens and the earth but comes to al-Raḥmān as a servant" (Q 19:93). Elsewhere in the chapter "servant" renders *ʿabd*, the usual correlate of *rabb*.

[^DH]: *Dhāt* is rendered "self," not the customary "essence." The word is originally "she who possesses," the bearer of the attributes, and in ordinary Arabic *bi-dhātihi* means simply "by itself": it names the thing's own self as against what it has or is related to. That is the contrast drawn here between the self, which needs no world, and lordship, which does. The adjective *dhātī* is rendered "intrinsic" where it is the logicians' term opposed to accident (see n. 27). "Essence" is kept for *māhiyya*, the "what-it-is" of a thing. *Nafs*, the other Arabic word that can mean "self," is rendered "soul" (see n. 5).

[^6]: Ibn ʿArabī often cites a ḥadīth in which the Prophet speaks of finding "the breath of al-Raḥmān" coming from the direction of Yemen. In his cosmology the "Breath of the All-Compassionate" is the medium in which all things are articulated, as words are articulated in breath.

[^7]: A ḥadīth in Muslim's *Ṣaḥīḥ* (with parallels in al-Bukhārī) describes God appearing to people on the Day of Resurrection in a form they do not recognize, so that they deny him, and then transforming into a form they do recognize.

[^8]: *Tajallī* is rendered "autophany," self-showing. The root means to polish, to make clear, to come into view; the reflexive verb means that God shows *himself*. It is distinct from emanation (*fayḍ*): its concern is visibility, the hidden becoming seen. The verb is rendered "shows himself" or "reveals himself" as context allows.

[^9]: *Faṣṣ*, the word in the book's title, is the stone set in a signet ring; *maḥall* is its socket or setting, which English jewellers call the bezel. The heart is the setting and the autophany the stone: the heart has no shape of its own beyond the shape of what fills it. The same image governs the book's title, in which each chapter is a "ringstone" set in the "word" of a prophet.

[^10]: *Huwiyya* is an abstract noun built from the pronoun *huwa*, "he" or "it": literally "he-ness." It is rendered here by a phrase, "what 'he' refers to," "who 'he' is," and the like, since no single English noun does its work: "being" suggests *wujūd*, and "identity" suggests a relation. A pronoun, unlike a name, refers without describing, so the *huwiyya* is God as referred to with nothing said about him, in contrast to God as named by the names, each of which implies a relation to the world. Arabic grammar also calls the third person "the absent one" (*al-ghāʾib*): to say "he" is to point to one who is not present. Hence the link to the unseen (*ghayb*) and to the "autophany of the unseen." God's self-designation "he" is Qurʾānic (Q 112:1, "Say: he, God, is One"). The quoted pronoun is lowercase like the others: the same word is said of God and of a person (see the passage at n. 23). In the Ashʿarite passage below, where the word has its philosophers' sense of a thing's identity, it is rendered "the thing itself."

[^11]: *Iʿtiqād* (believing) and *muʿtaqad* (what is believed) come from a root meaning "to tie a knot," and are rendered "belief" and "conception." The point is not that God is a mere idea, but that what each person sees of God has the shape of what they have tied themselves to (see n. 20). The final clause could also be read "he [the seer] is his very belief"; the primary reading is that what is seen is the belief, but the chapter's conclusion ("he is the one who shows himself and the one to whom he is shown") makes the second reading less strange than it first appears.

[^12]: The threefold repetition stands in the edition used and seems deliberate, enacting the unending request.

[^13]: From the *ḥadīth qudsī* of supererogatory works (al-Bukhārī): "My servant keeps drawing near to me through supererogatory works until I love him; and when I love him, I am his hearing with which he hears, his sight with which he sees, his hand with which he strikes, and his foot with which he walks." Ibn ʿArabī quotes it freely.

[^14]: The poem turns on near-untranslatable words. *Thamma* means "there"; the first line can also be read "who is there, when nothing is there?" *ʿAmma* and *khaṣṣa* ("include in common," "single out") echo the opening's "language of the common folk" and "language of the elect." *ʿAyn* means both "entity" and "eye": "no entity but the one entity" is also "no eye but the one eye," and the light of that eye is darkness, since the light by which all things are seen cannot itself be seen. The last two lines rhyme *ghumma*, "gloom" (literally, being covered over), with *himma*, "concentrated intent," from a root (*hamm*) meaning care or preoccupation: concern scattered and clouded set against concern gathered to a single point. In the Isaac chapter (ch. 6) Ibn ʿArabī says that the one who recognizes creates by *himma* things that exist outside himself, sustained only so long as he does not become heedless. Compare one-pointed concentration in other contemplative traditions.

[^15]: *ʿAql*, "intellect," is related to *ʿiqāl*, the cord that hobbles a camel. Intellect "binds": it fixes the matter to one description. The same binding vocabulary (*qayd*, *taqyīd*) describes those who "bind" God to their beliefs.

[^16]: A saying transmitted as a ḥadīth, ordinarily meaning "the people of kindness in this world are the people of kindness in the next." *Maʿrūf* means both "kindness, the good" and "the known, the recognized"; its opposite *munkar* means both "the reprehensible" and "the denied." Ibn ʿArabī reads the ethical saying ontologically: those who recognize the Real here recognize him there.

[^17]: *Ḥaḍra* is a king's "presence," the court one is admitted into; *ḥuḍūr*, being present, is the opposite of absence (*ghayba*). Where Ibn ʿArabī elsewhere speaks of the "world" (*ʿālam*) of imagination as a realm with its own inhabitants, *ḥaḍra* names the same domain as the place where the absent becomes present. Imagination (*khayāl*) here is not fancy but a real intermediate plane on which the unseen takes form.

[^18]: From the "ḥadīth of Gabriel" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim), which defines *iḥsān*, "excellence" or "doing what is beautiful": "that you worship God as if you see him; and if you do not see him, he sees you." That God is "in the prayer-direction of the one who prays" echoes another ḥadīth (al-Bukhārī): when one prays, God is before one's face.

[^19]: The prophet Shuʿayb, often identified with Jethro, bears a name from the root that gives *shuʿba*, "branch," and *tashaʿʿub*, "branching." Ibn ʿArabī's assignment of wisdoms to prophets often turns on such etymologies.

[^20]: *ʿUqda*, "knot," shares the root of *iʿtiqād*, "belief." The form seen does not dissolve — "it is real." What changes is how it is held: the knot of belief is untied into the knowledge of one who sees. The sharpened sight echoes Q 50:22: "We have lifted your covering from you, and your sight today is sharp."

[^21]: In the *Kitāb al-Tajalliyāt* Ibn ʿArabī recounts visionary encounters with earlier Sufis, among them al-Junayd and al-Ḥallāj, whom he instructs on points they had not grasped.

[^22]: Said of the fruits of paradise, which resemble those given before. Each autophany resembles the last closely enough that the ascent goes unnoticed.

[^23]: "Whoever knows (recognizes) his soul knows (recognizes) his Lord": a saying much cited by Sufis and often attributed to the Prophet, though not found in the canonical collections.

[^24]: "God created Adam in his form" (al-Bukhārī, Muslim). The pronoun "his" is ambiguous in the Arabic as in English.

[^25]: Two proverbs: mistaking a swelling for healthy fat (an image made famous by a line of al-Mutanabbī) and blowing on what is not a fire.

[^26]: The heresiographers describe the Ḥisbāniyya as sceptics, or "Sophists," who held that things are in perpetual flux and that truth follows opinion (*ḥusbān*, "supposition").

[^27]: The argument runs: the Ashʿarite theologians hold that the world consists of indivisible substances (*jawhar*) bearing accidents (*ʿaraḍ*), and that accidents do not endure two moments but are re-created by God at every instant. They define substance as "what occupies space and receives accidents." But occupying space and receiving are themselves accidents, since neither exists on its own; and an intrinsic definition is not something added to the thing defined but is the thing itself. So by their own definition substance is a bundle of accidents and should not endure two moments either. Without noticing, they have made the transient endure and the dependent self-subsistent. Ibn ʿArabī accepts their account of accidents and extends it to the whole world: everything is created anew with every breath, within a single entity.
